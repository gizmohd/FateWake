# Characters

## Maya Torres
Neighbor and nurse. Practical, capable and protective. Her first impression of the player can vary significantly based on Day 1.

## Eli Torres
Maya's eight-year-old son. Observant and prone to noticing details adults dismiss. He remembers player behavior.

## Marcus Reed
Former military/logistics background. Highly useful during collapse conditions but increasingly comfortable with authoritarian solutions.

## Dr. Lena Park
Communications engineer who suspects the outage and broadcasts do not fit official explanations.

## Jonah Mercer
Charismatic survivor who organizes people unusually quickly. His motivations and knowledge remain uncertain.

## Noah
Teenager encountered stealing food on Day 3. He claims to be caring for his younger sister. Their parents vanished during The Silence rather than being found dead.

## Character Rule
Major characters are not purely good or evil. Depending on player history and world state, they may live, die, leave, betray, love, hate, rescue, oppose or remember the player. Important NPCs should not automatically receive plot armor.

## Emergent Archetypes and Gifts
Fatewake does not begin with conventional class selection. The system observes behavior, aptitudes, relationships, Wakes and repeated choices. Identity and archetype emerge from what the player actually does.

The deeper shared cosmology retains the Wayfinder foundations of **Traveler, Listener and Builder**, but Fatewake cultures may use different names and may recognize partial, blended or Realm-specific manifestations before anyone understands their origin.

Gift development is rare. Most survivors remain ordinary humans whose expertise, leadership, relationships and history can be just as consequential as supernatural or Echo-related ability. Medicine, engineering, logistics, diplomacy, scouting, trade, combat and community leadership are valid high-impact paths.

A player who begins manifesting a Gift is not initially shown statistics such as `Listener +3`. The experience changes diegetically: static contains a voice others cannot hear, a route feels wrong before evidence explains why, an object seems unusually anchored to a memory, or a player recognizes structure in a Wake that others perceive only as coincidence.

Only later may characters—and eventually the player—gain language for what is happening. Becoming Wayfinder-like is therefore a narrative discovery produced by accumulated history, not a character-build reward.
